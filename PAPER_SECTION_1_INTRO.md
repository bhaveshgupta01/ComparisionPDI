# Section 1 — Introduction (paper draft, ~0.75 page)

**Drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction** narrows a search space of ~10^9 small molecules to a tractable shortlist for wet-lab assay. The downstream economics — billions of dollars per drug program, multi-year timelines — rest on whether the predictor's ranking is trustworthy. Modern transformer-based DTI models routinely report concordance index ≥ 0.85 on standard benchmarks (BindingDB Ki, Davis, KIBA), and the field has converged on a striking architectural consensus: **encode drug and protein independently, then fuse the two representations late**, typically by concatenation or cross-attention, before a small MLP head. Surveying the recent literature — MolTrans [Huang21], DeepPurpose [Huang20], HyperAttentionDTI [Zhao22], PerceiverCPI [Nguyen22], TransformerCPI [Chen20], DrugBAN [Bai23], FusionDTI — every system follows this pattern. The choice is folklore: it appears in no paper as a justified design decision, and to our knowledge no controlled study has audited it.

We ask the most direct question this default invites: **at what stage of the network should the drug and protein representations meet?** Two design axes define the answer space:
- *Fusion stage*: before the encoder body (drug and protein interact at the input level) or after (each modality is encoded independently, then aggregated)?
- *Fusion mechanism*: simple concatenation, or attention across the two streams?

These axes form a 2 × 2 matrix of canonical variants — V1 Early Concatenation, V2 Early Cross-Attention, V3 Late Cross-Attention, V4 Late Concatenation — that span the design space. The field default is V3 or V4 (late fusion); V1 and V2 (early fusion) are essentially absent from the published transformer-DTI literature.

We train all four variants under matched hyperparameters and identical data, varying only the fusion choice, and run the comparison at three model widths (`d_model` ∈ {128, 192, 256}) keeping every other axis fixed. **We discover a scale-dependent phase transition.** At small capacity (d=128), early-fusion variants win all three evaluation splits — the field default is the worst architecture by every metric. At larger capacity (d=256), the ranking reverses: late-fusion variants win every split, and the simplest possible architecture (V4, mean-pool each modality, concatenate, MLP) wins the cold-drug split. The intermediate scale (d=192) brackets the transition.

The mechanism is straightforward in retrospect: late fusion requires *two* per-modality encoders that each carry sufficient information about their respective stream. At small capacity, neither encoder alone is rich enough, so cross-modal interaction must happen earlier. At large capacity, the encoders compress enough signal that the simplest aggregation suffices and the bias toward early interaction disappears. This pattern matches well-documented capacity-versus-aggregation tradeoffs in vision (CLS-pool vs cross-attention heads) and language modeling (mean-pool vs attention pooling), but has not been characterised for DTI — where it has direct economic consequences. A paper that reports an architecture as best at one scale may be reporting the *opposite* of what holds at production scale.

**Contributions.** (i) A controlled 2 × 2 × 3-split × 5-seed comparison at d=256 (60 runs); a matched 2 × 2 × 3-split × 3-seed comparison at d=128 (36 runs); and an intermediate-scale 2 × 2 × 3-split × 3-seed comparison at d=192 (36 runs) — all on full BindingDB Ki, identical optimizer/schedule/data. (ii) A second-axis ablation at d=128, n_layers=12 (12 runs) decomposing whether the reversal is driven by width or by total capacity. (iii) Mechanistic evidence at both scales: layer- and head-level causal ablations on Phase C checkpoints, mask-aware attention entropy, and CKA-based representation similarity. (iv) A fully-reproducible training and analysis pipeline released with the paper, totaling ~165 GPU-hours on one A100 partition.

The headline takeaway: **the architecture-level question "where should drug and protein meet?" has a quantitatively different answer at different scales, and current DTI papers do not control for this.**
