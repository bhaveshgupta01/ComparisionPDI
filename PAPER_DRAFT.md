## Abstract (≤150 words)

Predicting whether a drug binds a protein target — drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction — is the load-bearing primitive of modern computational drug discovery. Before COVID-19, validating one drug-protein pair in the wet lab took 7-10 days; the pandemic forced the field to lean heavily on machine-learning surrogates, with transformer architectures emerging as the dominant approach. The field has converged on a single design pattern — *encode drug and protein independently, fuse the two representations late* — without controlled justification. We construct a 2 × 2 ablation that isolates the two design choices: (i) *fusion stage* (before or after the encoder body) and (ii) *fusion mechanism* (concatenation or cross-attention). Training all four variants under identical hyperparameters across four model configurations on BindingDB Ki, we discover a **width-specific phase transition**: at small width (`d_model = 128`), early-fusion cross-attention wins every split; at larger width (`d_model = 256`), late-fusion variants take over and the field-default Late-Concatenation baseline becomes the best architecture on the cold-drug split. **The transition is not driven by total capacity** — doubling depth at `d=128` does not reproduce the reversal. The mechanism, supported by attention-entropy, CKA, and causal layer/head ablations, is consistent with a per-modality bottleneck story.

## 1. Introduction (~0.75 page)

### 1.1 Why DTI prediction matters

Drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction asks the deceptively simple question: *given a small molecule and a protein target, does this molecule bind, and how strongly?* The answer determines whether a candidate drug enters expensive validation. **Before COVID-19, answering it for a single pair via wet-lab assay took 7-10 days** — competent labs ran dose-response curves, IC50 / Ki assays, and selectivity panels in series. With ~10⁹ commercially-available compounds and ~20,000 human protein targets, a brute-force wet-lab campaign is impossible.

The COVID-19 pandemic compressed the timeline. With repurposing pipelines, virtual screens, and target-based drug design under public scrutiny, machine-learning surrogates were no longer a research curiosity — they became deployment infrastructure. A model that returns a binding-affinity prediction in microseconds, trained on the public corpus of historical assay data (BindingDB, Davis, KIBA), became the obvious replacement for serial wet-lab triage. The first AI-designed small molecule entered Phase I trials (Exscientia / Sumitomo's DSP-1181) in 2020 — collapsing the 4-5 year traditional hit-to-clinic interval to under 12 months. Insilico Medicine's INS018_055 (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) reached Phase II in 2024.

### 1.2 Why transformers won

Early ML approaches to DTI used 1-D convolutional networks over SMILES strings and protein sequences (DeepDTA, DeepConv-DTI). These captured local sequence patterns but plateaued at ~85% concordance index on standard benchmarks because **the convolutional inductive bias only models token *order locally*; it cannot directly express long-range token-to-token interactions** between, say, a particular SMILES atom and a distant binding-pocket residue. Recurrent networks (LSTMs, GRUs) captured order but compressed everything through a fixed-size hidden state — also an information bottleneck.

Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017) broke through both limits. *Self-attention* lets every token attend to every other token directly; *positional encoding* lets the model see absolute token positions explicitly. On DTI benchmarks, transformer-based predictors (MolTrans, DeepPurpose, HyperAttentionDTI, PerceiverCPI, FusionDTI, TransformerCPI, DrugBAN) routinely outperform CNN baselines by 2-5 CI points and have become the de-facto architecture family.

### 1.3 The folklore choice

But transformers don't dictate *where* drug and protein representations should meet. Surveying the recent transformer-DTI literature reveals a striking architectural consensus: **encode drug and protein independently, fuse the resulting representations late** — typically by concatenating mean-pooled vectors or applying a cross-attention block after independent encoding. The choice appears in no paper as a justified design decision. Some authors choose concatenation, others cross-attention; some fuse after the encoder, others before; some use single encoders, others dual. **No published controlled comparison isolates fusion stage as the variable.** It is folklore — inherited not justified.

This is consequential. A wrong default locked into the field has compounding cost: every downstream paper inherits it, every benchmark report does, every commercial pipeline does. Even if the default is *only sometimes* wrong, knowing *when* it is wrong has economic weight.

### 1.4 What we do

We construct four canonical transformer DTI variants — **V1 Early Concatenation**, **V2 Early Cross-Attention**, **V3 Late Cross-Attention**, **V4 Late Concatenation** — that span the 2 × 2 design matrix of *fusion stage* × *fusion mechanism*. We hold every other architectural decision fixed (encoder block, optimizer, schedule, head, data) and train all four variants at four model configurations: small (`d=128, n_layers=6/3`), depth-doubled (`d=128, n_layers=12/6`), mid-width (`d=192`), and width-doubled (`d=256`).

Across 144 controlled training runs on full BindingDB Ki, we find:

1. **A scale-dependent reversal** of the early-vs-late ranking. At `d=128`, V2 (Early X-Attn) wins; at `d=256`, V3 (Late X-Attn) wins (paired-seed t-test on random split: p = 0.010). **The field-default architecture (V4 Late Concat) — which we initially dismissed as "never optimal" at small scale — wins the cold-drug split at d=256.**
2. **The reversal is width-specific.** Doubling depth at `d=128` (n_layers=12/6) does *not* reverse the ranking — V2 still wins. Only width-doubling (d=256) flips the winner. Late variants gain ≈ 3× more from doubling width than from doubling depth.
3. **Mechanistic evidence** for a per-modality information-bottleneck story: V1's encoder shows depth-dependent attention specialization (entropy dip from 5.95 nats at layer 0 to 5.15 nats at layer 4), V2's does not — the cross-attention block at V2's input has already done the cross-modal alignment work. CKA on attention features at d=128 shows two clusters by fusion stage ({V1, V2} CKA = 0.98; {V3, V4} CKA = 0.93); at d=256 the early cluster weakens (0.76) while the late cluster stays tight (0.87) — i.e. early variants *diverge* with width while late variants *converge*.
4. **Representation-swap evidence**: replacing V3's drug encoder with V4's improves V3's MSE by −0.39 (a 20% drop). V3 has co-adapted with a sub-optimal drug encoder; the cross-attention block compensates, but a better encoder would let it perform substantially better.

### 1.5 Contributions

- **A controlled 2 × 2 × 4-config × multi-seed experiment** (144 training runs, ~190 GPU-hours on NYU HPC) that isolates fusion stage and mechanism from the confounds normally bundled into DTI architecture comparisons.
- **Identification of a width-specific phase transition** in transformer fusion strategy for DTI — distinct from the more common "scale-dependent" framing — and the practical implication that DTI architecture papers should report at least two scales for any architecture-comparison claim.
- **Mechanistic dissection** of the reversal via mask-aware attention entropy, CKA, and causal layer/head/representation-swap ablations.
- **Open-source pipeline** (data preprocessing, training, evaluation, analysis, all 11 figures) released with the paper.

## 2. Method (~1.0 page)

### 2.1 Four variants, one axis of variation

We construct four transformer DTI predictors that share embedding tables, transformer encoder block, optimizer, schedule, loss, and prediction head. **The only thing that changes across the four variants is where and how drug and protein representations interact.**

|  | **Concatenation** | **Cross-Attention** |
|---|---|---|
| **Before encoding (early)** | **V1** Early Concat | **V2** Early Cross-Attn |
| **After encoding (late)**  | **V4** Late Concat (field default) | **V3** Late Cross-Attn |

- **V1 Early Concatenation.** Concatenate token embeddings: `[CLS, T_d(drug), SEP, T_p(prot)]`. Pass through a single shared encoder body. Pool `[CLS]`. Single shared encoder; cheapest at inference.
- **V2 Early Cross-Attention.** Run a bidirectional cross-attention block between embedded drug and protein streams *before* a shared encoder consumes the fused stream. Pool `[CLS]`.
- **V3 Late Cross-Attention.** Two separate encoders. After encoding, a bidirectional cross-attention block exchanges information; mean-pool each side; concatenate; MLP head.
- **V4 Late Concatenation.** Two separate encoders. Mean-pool each side; concatenate; MLP head. The field-default DTI architecture.

### 2.2 Shared scaffolding

| Component | Choice |
|---|---|
| Encoder block | Pre-norm Transformer, GELU FFN |
| Attention | `nn.MultiheadAttention`, `batch_first=True` |
| Position encoding | Sinusoidal |
| Drug tokenizer | SMILES regex-based; vocab 58-66 (built from training corpus) |
| Protein tokenizer | Char-level: 20 amino acids + special tokens (vocab 24) |
| Length caps | Drug ≤ 100 tokens (covers 83%); protein ≤ 1200 residues (covers 99%) |
| Pooling | Mean over non-pad tokens (or `[CLS]` for V1/V2) |
| Head | 2-layer MLP, GELU, dropout 0.1, scalar pKi |
| Loss | MSE on pKi |
| Optimizer | AdamW, β = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay 1e-2 |
| LR schedule | Cosine, 500-step warmup |
| Reproducibility | Seeds set on Python / NumPy / PyTorch / cuDNN; `cudnn.benchmark=False` |

### 2.3 Four model configurations

| Phase | `d_model` | `n_heads` | `d_ff` | `n_layers` (V1/V2 shared, V3/V4 per-side) | `head_hidden` | `bs` | epochs | seeds | jobs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C** (baseline) | 128 | 4 | 512  | 6, 3+3 | 256 | 64 | 30 | 3 | 36 |
| **E6** (depth × 2) | 128 | 4 | 512  | 12, 6+6 | 256 | 32-64 | 30 | 3 | 12 |
| **E1b** (mid width) | 192 | 6 | 768  | 6, 3+3 | 384 | 32 | 45 | 3 | 36 |
| **E1** (width × 2) | 256 | 8 | 1024 | 6, 3+3 | 256 | 32 | 45 | 5 | 60 |

`d_ff = 4 · d_model` and `d_head = 32 = d_model / n_heads` are preserved across configurations. Seeds at each config are *identical across variants*, enabling paired-seed t-tests. **Total: 144 controlled training runs.**

### 2.4 Data

**BindingDB PSDPKi** (Liu et al., 2023). Filter: valid `Ki (nM)`, valid SMILES, valid sequence; 27,715 raw rows → 21,382 valid pairs. Target: `pKi = −log₁₀(Ki / 10⁹)`, clipped to [3, 12]; 21% censored at pKi=5.0 (treated as point measurements). Length truncations as described in §2.2.

### 2.5 Splits

- **Random 80/10/10** by pair (sanity check; both drug and target may appear in train).
- **Cold-drug**: drugs in test never seen in train (tests generalization to new chemistry).
- **Cold-target**: targets in test never seen in train (hardest; tests generalization to new biology).

Splits deterministic per seed; seed-paired across variants at each config.

### 2.6 Reporting

We report **best validation MSE** per run (mean ± std across seeds) and additionally compute **test-set MSE, concordance index** (Davis et al., 2011) and **Pearson r** by post-hoc inference on each Phase E1 checkpoint (60 evaluations). **Significance tests** are paired-seed t-tests (n = 5 at d=256, n = 3 elsewhere). All raw numbers in committed CSVs; all figures auto-generated.

### 2.7 Mechanistic analyses

- **Mask-aware attention entropy** per layer per variant on a 256-pair held-out batch — average only over valid (non-pad) query positions.
- **Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)** (Kornblith et al., 2019) on attention-entropy features across variants at both `d=128` and `d=256`.
- **Causal ablations** on Phase C random-split seed=42 checkpoints: zero each `nn.MultiheadAttention` block (one at a time) and measure ΔMSE; same for individual heads; **representation-swap** (V3 ↔ V4 drug encoders).

### 2.8 Compute

NYU HPC Cloud Bursting cluster, single-A100 (40 GB) jobs on partition `c12m85-a100-1`. Wall-clock per run: Phase C ≈ 28 min, E6 ≈ 50-110 min, E1b ≈ 110 min, E1 ≈ 150 min. **Total compute footprint: ~190 GPU-hours.**

**At Phase C (d=128), V2 wins random and cold-target; V3 wins cold-drug. At Phase E1 (d=256), V3 wins random and cold-target; V4 wins cold-drug.** The early→late winner shift is not within seed noise: paired-seed t-test on V3 vs V2 at d=256 random gives **p = 0.010** (mean Δ = −0.037).

### 3.2 The phase transition is gradual

We trained the same 2×2 set at an intermediate width `d_model = 192` (Phase E1b). Figure 4 plots random-split val MSE vs `d_model`. The V3 / V2 crossover (the canonical reversal indicator) **occurs between d=128 and d=192, not between d=192 and d=256**: at d=192 V3 already leads V2 by 0.036. The transition is smooth, not sharp.

> **Figure 4.** Capacity curve, x-axis `d_model ∈ {128, 192, 256}`, y-axis val MSE, four lines (one per variant) with ±1 std ribbons.

### 3.3 The reversal is width-specific, not capacity-driven

Phase E6 holds width at `d=128` and doubles depth (n_layers 6/3 → 12/6) — making the model approximately as parameter-rich as Phase E1's (d=256, n=6/3) along the depth axis instead of the width axis. Figure 9 shows the comparison.

| Variant | Phase C (d=128, n=6/3) | E6 depth × 2 (d=128, n=12/6) | E1 width × 2 (d=256, n=6/3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1 EC | 1.004 ± 0.031 | 0.988 ± 0.025 | 0.824 ± 0.031 |
| V2 EX | **0.948 ± 0.023** ★ | **0.910 ± 0.020** ★ | 0.784 ± 0.038 |
| V3 LX | 1.030 ± 0.039 | 0.949 ± 0.041 | **0.747 ± 0.030** ★ |
| V4 LC | 1.119 ± 0.018 | 0.971 ± 0.034 | 0.753 ± 0.032 |

**V2 (Early X-Attn) wins under depth-doubling at the same width**; **V3 only wins after width-doubling.** Late variants also benefit *more* from width than depth: width-doubling drops late-variant MSE by 0.325 on average vs only 0.114 from depth-doubling — a **~3× greater effect for width**. **The reversal is a property of the width axis, not total capacity.**

> **Figure 9.** Width-vs-depth bar chart. Three columns per variant: Phase C / E6 (depth×2) / E1 (width×2). Winners marked. V2 wins under depth-doubling; V3 wins under width-doubling.

### 3.4 Per-variant differential improvement under scaling

Late variants gain ~25% on random split when going from d=128 to d=256; early variants only ~17%. The differential is consistent across cold splits (Figure 5). Width-scaling is *2×* more effective for late variants than for early variants — this is the immediate mechanism behind the reversal.

> **Figure 5.** Bar chart, Δ % MSE per (variant, split), Phase C → E1.

### 3.5 Mechanistic dissection

#### 3.5.1 Mask-aware attention entropy

Pad-token positions inflate per-layer attention entropy in standard analyses. Recomputing while averaging only over valid query positions: **V1 mean entropy 5.79 nats; V2 mean entropy 5.95 nats**. V1 dips to 5.15 nats at layer 4; V2 stays flat across all six layers (5.88-5.97).

> **Figure 6.** Entropy curve, two-panel (pad-contaminated vs mask-aware), V1 (blue) vs V2 (orange) over six encoder layers.

V1 develops *depth-dependent attention specialization* — the encoder body works progressively harder at cross-modal alignment. V2's encoder body does not — the cross-attention block at V2's input has already done the alignment work. This explains *why* V2 outperforms V1 at small width.

#### 3.5.2 Causal layer / head ablation

We zero each `nn.MultiheadAttention` block on the Phase C seed=42 random-split checkpoint and measure ΔMSE. Most-load-bearing layers per variant:

- V1 EC: **encoder layer 4** (Δ = +0.110) — confirms the layer-4 entropy-dip finding
- V2 EX: encoder layer 0 (Δ = +0.035) — V2's load is more distributed
- V3/V4: protein-encoder layer 0 (Δ ≈ 0); ablating drug encoder layers actually *improves* MSE for V3 (Δ = −0.505) — V3's drug encoder is a liability

> **Figure 7.** Layer-ablation heatmap (rows = variants, cols = layer index, color = ΔMSE).

#### 3.5.3 V3 ↔ V4 representation swap

Replacing V3's drug encoder with V4's drops V3 MSE by 0.394 (V3: 1.974 → 1.580). The reverse swap (V3's drug encoder into V4) is approximately neutral (Δ = +0.037). **V3's drug encoder is co-adapted with a sub-optimal cross-attention block in a way that hurts performance**; V4's "boring" mean-pool drug encoder is generic enough to plug into V3 with zero degradation.

> **Figure 7c.** Bar chart of baseline vs swapped MSE for V3 and V4.

#### 3.5.4 Cross-variant representation similarity (CKA)

| Pair | C (d=128) CKA | E1 (d=256) CKA |
|---|---|---|
| Within early-fusion {V1, V2} | 0.976 | 0.761 |
| Within late-fusion {V3, V4}  | 0.928 | 0.869 |
| Across stages, mean          | 0.578 | 0.606 |

**Late-fusion variants stay coherent under width scaling** (CKA drops only 6%); **early-fusion variants diverge** (CKA drops 22%). At small width, both families operate on a shared "compressed cross-modal feature" mode; at larger width, late variants converge on a stable solution while early variants explore a wider region of feature space.

> **Figure 8.** Two-panel CKA heatmap: d=128 vs d=256.

### 3.6 Statistical significance (Appendix A)

Paired-seed t-test (n = 5 at d=256). Random split:

- V3 vs V2: mean Δ = −0.037, **p = 0.010** (V3 wins, significant).
- V4 vs V1: mean Δ = −0.071, **p = 0.010** (V4 wins, significant — late-vs-early is significant for the concat pair).
- V3 vs V4: mean Δ = −0.006, p = 0.385 (the two late variants are statistically indistinguishable).

Cold splits do not reach p < 0.05 between best-pair candidates due to seed variance (cold-drug V4 vs V3 p = 0.42; cold-target V3 vs V2 p = 0.11). The significant findings are concentrated on the random split, where seed variance is lowest.

## 4. Discussion (~0.5 page)

### 4.1 Why width — and only width — reverses the ranking

Late fusion factorizes the problem into (encode-drug) → (encode-protein) → (combine). Each per-modality encoder must compress *enough* signal that the late combiner can recover binding affinity from the two pooled vectors. This is fundamentally an **information-bottleneck story at the encoder output**: the per-modality compressed representation is a fixed-size vector. *Widening* the encoder enlarges this representation linearly with `d_model`. *Deepening* the encoder does not: more layers refine the same fixed-size representation but cannot expand it. **The bottleneck is per-modality representational capacity, which is the width axis specifically.**

Early fusion does not face this bottleneck: drug and protein tokens mix at the input level and the encoder operates on the combined stream throughout. The price of early fusion is that the same encoder parameters must learn both per-modality compression *and* cross-modal alignment — a dual workload. At small width, the dual workload is too much; the early-fusion encoder can't both compress *and* align well, so V1 and V2 underperform. At larger width, the dual workload becomes affordable, but no longer outperforms the (now bottleneck-loosened) late variants.

This account predicts what we observe: late variants' improvement from width-doubling (~25%) > their improvement from depth-doubling (~10%); early variants improve roughly equally on both axes (~17%, ~3% respectively). It also predicts the CKA pattern — late variants converge on the same compressed solution as width grows (within-cluster CKA stays high), early variants diverge (their dual-workload solutions are less stable across seeds).

### 4.2 Mechanistic picture

The four variants' mechanistic profiles support distinct roles for each architectural choice:

- **V1 (Early Concat)** uses depth-dependent attention specialization (entropy dip at layer 4) to sharpen cross-modal alignment. Its layer-4 ablation hurts most. This is the "do the dual workload by sequencing the steps" strategy.
- **V2 (Early X-Attn)** outsources cross-modal alignment to its dedicated cross-attention block — the encoder body is left with per-modality compression, hence the flat entropy curve. Layer-0 head ablation hurts most (the cross-attention block is up-front).
- **V3 (Late X-Attn)** has dual encoders that should each do per-modality compression, with the cross-attention block aligning their pooled outputs. **V3's drug encoder is co-adapted with a sub-optimal cross-attention block** — swapping in V4's drug encoder improves MSE by 20%. The cross-attention block is doing *too much* work, masking a weaker drug encoder.
- **V4 (Late Concat)** has the simplest design and the lowest seed variance — at small width it's last because it lacks any cross-modal mixing, but at d=256 the dual encoders compress enough that simple concatenation suffices. **V4 is the field-default architecture, and at production scale, the field is right** — for the random and cold-drug splits at least.

### 4.3 Practical consequences

1. **DTI architecture papers underspecify scale.** When two methods report different numbers, the gap may be explained by scale rather than by mechanism. We recommend reporting at least two scales for any architecture claim, and **at least one width-axis comparison** since width — not depth — is what triggers the reversal we identify.
2. **The field default (V4) is the right answer at production scale.** The published consensus on architecture is correct *for production-scale deployments* — but past comparisons against early-fusion baselines were probably done at smaller scales where the bias is reversed. Reading those head-to-head numbers as architectural truths is therefore unsafe.
3. **For inference-cost-constrained deployments** (e.g. virtual screening at 10⁹ compounds), V1 dominates V4 in inference cost (one shared encoder vs two) at *similar accuracy at d=256*. We recommend V1 as a serious candidate the field has overlooked.
4. **For interpretability / wet-lab handoff**, V2's dedicated cross-attention block is the single most-load-bearing circuit and the natural site for binding-pocket attribution analyses. V3's apparent gain from cross-attention may be a co-adaptation artifact rather than a genuine architectural advantage.

## 5. Limitations (~0.25 page)

- **Single dataset.** BindingDB Ki only. Davis (kinase-narrow) and KIBA (broader chemistry) cross-checks are deferred — we cite the standard caveat that BindingDB is ~50% kinase-family targets and the reversal may have a weaker signal on more diverse benchmarks.
- **Sequence-only inputs.** No molecular graph (GNN), no 3D protein structure (AlphaFold), no pretrained encoders (ChemBERTa, ESM-2). Whether pretraining shifts or eliminates the phase boundary is an open question; we hypothesize it shifts the boundary *down* (smaller models suffice with a stronger inductive prior) but do not test.
- **Three width points + one depth point.** Three scales (d ∈ {128, 192, 256}) bracket the transition but do not characterize its sharpness; a denser sweep is straightforward. The depth-axis ablation has only one extra point (n=12/6 at d=128); the depth axis could be richer.
- **Combined width × depth scaling not run.** Whether the late-fusion advantage amplifies, plateaus, or re-reverses at (d=256, n=12/6) is open. We did not run this configuration because of memory constraints and budget priorities; future work should fill it in.
- **Censored labels.** 21% of pKi labels are censored at 5.0; we treat as point measurements. Censoring-aware regression would shift quantitative numbers but is unlikely to flip the architecture ranking.
- **Statistical power on cold splits.** With 3-5 seeds, paired t-tests on cold splits do not reach p < 0.05 between best-pair candidates. The random-split reversal is statistically robust; cold-split rankings are descriptive.

## 6. Conclusion (~0.2 page)

We ran the first controlled comparison of fusion stage × mechanism in transformer DTI predictors, varying only the architectural axis we sought to study and holding everything else fixed across 144 runs at four configurations. The result is that the field's tacit consensus on fusion architecture is **scale-dependent — and specifically width-dependent**. At small width (d=128), early-fusion cross-attention wins; at production-scale width (d=256), late-fusion variants take over and the field's default architecture is the best choice on the cold-drug split. Doubling depth at the same width does not produce the reversal — the effect is on the width axis, not total capacity. The mechanism, supported by attention-entropy, CKA, and causal ablations, is consistent with a per-modality information-bottleneck story.

This reframes how DTI architecture papers should be read: a single-scale comparison of two architectures is necessary but not sufficient. **The unit of evidence required to justify an architectural design choice is a scale-explicit comparison along the width axis.** We release the full pipeline at github.com/bhaveshgupta01/ComparisionPDI.
